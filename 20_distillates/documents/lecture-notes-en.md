# Distillate: Full Lecture Notes, English

The English Full Lecture Notes cover the same teaching line as the German version under prose headings and carry the material the German chapters leave out, the model-internal account from tokenisation to the Latent Program Space thesis, the model and harness landscape, the agent history before LLMs, and the four-part assessment vocabulary of evaluation, verification, validation and acceptance. Statements that the German version already carries in the same sense are not repeated here; the parallel distillate is [[20_distillates/documents/lecture-notes-de]].

## Core statements

- Applied Generative AI denotes the application and adaptation of generative AI methods and systems to domain-specific problems, with the focus on integrating them into existing knowledge practices rather than on developing new foundation models. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0009]] ^e1
- Research data are analogue or digital representations of research objects, sources, observations or processes that are selected, collected, constructed or produced through scholarly work and require documentation, context and preservation to support scholarly claims. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0011]] ^e2
- A facsimile, a transcription and an encoded representation of the same historical document are not interchangeable, because each is a different representation with different affordances and different forms of uncertainty. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0012]] ^e3
- AI-ready data are data structured and documented for effective use in AI and machine learning workflows, including machine-readable formats and metadata, clear provenance and usage conditions, and transparent information about quality, uncertainty and potential bias. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0013]] ^e4
- Prompt Engineering is the iterative design and evaluation of instructions for a specific model and task, Knowledge Engineering creates, curates and maintains explicit knowledge for use by people and AI systems, Context Engineering is the systematic selection, organisation, maintenance and provision of the information an LLM-based system requires, and Agentic Engineering is the systematic organisation of extended work performed by AI agents. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0015]] ^e5
- Agentic capability arises from the combined system of model, harness and environment rather than from the model alone. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0016]] ^e6
- Research data workflows combine probabilistic operations, in which a model proposes a transcription, classification or structured representation, with deterministic operations, in which software transforms data, validates encodings, executes tests or checks numerical relations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0018]] ^e7
- The surrounding project environment is an epistemic infrastructure in which files, project knowledge, schemas, tests, provenance information, model outputs and editorial decisions together establish the conditions under which a generated representation can be inspected, criticised, validated and accepted for a particular purpose. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0019]] ^e8
- Model choice should not be reduced to benchmark performance, because systems differ in capability, modalities, inference cost, latency, openness, deployment options, context capacity, tool use and the harnesses through which they can operate. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0021]] ^e9
- The effective AI system is increasingly a compound system rather than a model considered in isolation, because harnesses connect models with files, tools, state and executable environments. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0022]] ^e10
- Leading figures of frontier AI companies have publicly described the automation of research as a strategic objective, and such statements are no evidence that research has been automated while indicating the direction in which leading laboratories are attempting to extend model capability. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0023]] ^e11
- Heterogeneous evaluations of task duration, adaptation to unfamiliar tasks, mathematical reasoning and behaviour in executable environments measure different things and should not be collapsed into one scale of intelligence. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0024]] ^e12
- Asymmetric amplification names the observation that frontier LLMs particularly amplify work where relevant information is digitally represented, actions can be executed through software and the environment can return useful feedback, and that the effect depends on existing expertise, accessible data, technical infrastructure and the ability to assess outputs. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0025]] ^e13
- The metaphor of jagged alien intelligence describes an unfamiliar capability profile in which frontier LLMs can perform extremely difficult tasks while failing on neighbouring tasks that appear simple, so their competence is uneven and difficult to extrapolate. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0026]] ^e14
- Model outputs are probabilistic and can contain plausible but unsupported claims, models can reproduce biases and display sycophancy as a tendency to align an answer with the belief a user has expressed, and their internal computations are only partially understood. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0027]] ^e15
- Mechanistic interpretability research indicates that model behaviour can depend on structured internal computational pathways that differ substantially from familiar human procedures, which motivates reading LLMs as systems in which training has produced a repertoire of learned transformations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0028]] ^e16
- The training objective is not identical to the capabilities acquired during training, because performing next-token prediction well across heterogeneous data requires representations and transformations associated with syntax, concepts, relations, styles, code and recurring patterns of reasoning. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0031]] ^e17
- The basic training objective does not ask whether a proposition is true and asks which continuation is probable given the available context, so language modelling can produce broad world representations while truth is not identical to the next-token objective. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0032]] ^e18
- Most contemporary LLMs are based on the Transformer architecture, which processes token representations through repeated layers in which attention mechanisms allow information at different positions in the sequence to influence one another. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0033]] ^e19
- Self-attention allows the model to construct context-dependent representations rather than treating every token independently, and the probability assigned to a continuation results from transformations across many layers and high-dimensional representations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0034]] ^e20
- Prompts and context alter the token sequence supplied to the model and thereby condition the distributed computation through which it produces subsequent tokens, so prompting is an intervention on the current computation rather than a textual wrapper around a fixed answer. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0035]] ^e21
- Describing pretraining as building a repertoire of knowledge and capabilities remains a simplification, because knowledge and capability are entangled throughout model training. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0036]] ^e22
- Contemporary development pipelines may include intermediate stages between broad pretraining and later alignment training, and the boundaries and terminology of those stages differ across laboratories and papers. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0037]] ^e23
- Ordinary inference does not normally update model weights, and the model can nevertheless adapt strongly to examples, instructions and information supplied in its current context. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0041]] ^e24
- An LLM does not normally retrieve an addressable copy of a training document when it answers a question, because training changes model parameters so that statistical structure from training data influences later generation, and what remains is a learned statistical structure rather than the original document. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0042]] ^e25
- A model may produce an accurate description because its parameters encode patterns learned from relevant material, and it cannot thereby provide direct provenance for every generated claim or reconstruct the exact source from which a statement was learned. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0043]] ^e26
- Three information layers should be distinguished, the learned representations in model parameters, the retrievable information in external resources, and the information available in the current context for this inference. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0044]] ^e27
- The boundary of the model is not the boundary of the complete AI system. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0048]] ^e28
- A tokenizer converts character sequences into discrete units called tokens, where one token may correspond to a word, part of a word, punctuation or another recurring sequence, and each token is mapped to a numerical identifier before it enters the network. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0049]] ^e29
- Tokenisation is partly an engineering decision that balances vocabulary size, sequence length and the ability to represent previously unseen strings, and the boundaries it creates are not necessarily linguistically intuitive. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0050]] ^e30
- Context windows, input costs and output lengths are measured in tokens rather than words or characters, so tokenisation determines the discrete sequence over which model computation and context limits operate. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0051]] ^e31
- Embeddings provide the initial mapping of discrete token identifiers into a high-dimensional vector space, and systematic relations can emerge during training among the representations associated with recurring linguistic and conceptual patterns. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0052]] ^e32
- The illustration in which related words appear closer together gives a first intuition and is not a complete account of meaning, because initial embeddings are transformed repeatedly across the network into contextual representations that depend on surrounding tokens and the current task. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0053]] ^e33
- Two semantically similar sentences written in different registers can condition different internal representations and activation patterns, which explains why wording matters without assuming that the model stores one fixed meaning per token. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0054]] ^e34
- Agentic work begins before the first substantive prompt, so the first decision is where the model is going to work rather than how an instruction is phrased. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0055]] ^e35
- Preparing the workspace determines which files, tools, knowledge and forms of feedback later become available to the model or agent, so the environment is part of the methodological design rather than neutral technical plumbing. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0057]] ^e36
- The notes advance three claims about prompting, that the engineering focus shifts from the isolated prompt towards Context Engineering, that no prompt rules them all because effects depend on model, version, task, context and evaluation, and that prompting can be understood as finding coordinates in a Latent Program Space. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0059]] ^e37
- Effects found for one generation of models may weaken, disappear or reverse for another, so prompting strategies should be treated as empirical interventions tied to particular model and evaluation conditions rather than as memorised tricks. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0062]] ^e38
- A generic expert persona adds no factual knowledge to the model, while roles can be useful where the task genuinely requires a particular perspective, audience, communicative style or evaluative frame. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0063]] ^e39
- A prompt treated as a bounded specification separates the immediate task, the source context, the transcription rules and the expected output. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0064]] ^e40
- The categories of a bounded specification can be changed independently, so a different source changes the context, a different editorial policy changes the rules and a different downstream workflow changes the output contract, which makes the arrangement a transparent separation rather than a universal template. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0066]] ^e41
- An instruction can require the model to preserve explicit uncertainty rather than resolve a difficult reading through linguistic plausibility, and whether the rule works has to be assessed against the source. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0071]] ^e42
- A generated transcription can appear remarkably fluent while individual readings remain wrong, so fluency, grammaticality and internal coherence are no evidence of fidelity to the source. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0073]] ^e43
- A model output should be treated as a candidate representation that requires comparison with the source, because plausibility is not equivalent to validation. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0074]] ^e44
- A Vision Language Model processes visual and linguistic information within the same task, so a general-purpose multimodal model can receive a facsimile together with an instruction and generate a transcription without being a dedicated recognition system. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0075]] ^e45
- A Vision Language Model does not merely recognise isolated characters, because visual patterns, layout, linguistic context and task instructions jointly influence the generated output, which creates the characteristic failure mode of errors that are contextually plausible rather than locally obvious. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0077]] ^e46
- Whether the ability to transcribe previously unseen handwriting should be classified as an emergent capability depends on the definition of emergence, on model scale and on the unknown composition of proprietary training data. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0078]] ^e47
- The Latent Program Space account claims that an LLM contains a large repertoire of learned computational transformations and that prompts act partly as signals which select and combine these transformations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0079]] ^e48
- A vector program is a distributed transformation implemented through high-dimensional representations and model parameters rather than a conventional symbolic program stored as a discrete object, and the thesis proposes that useful model behaviours correspond to learned computational structures that can be differentially elicited. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0086]] ^e49
- Iterative Prompt Engineering is an external search process in which a user varies a program query and evaluates the resulting behaviour. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0087]] ^e50
- Mechanistic interpretability investigates the internal computations through which neural networks produce behaviour, and attribution graph research has reconstructed partial internal pathways associated with multi-step computations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0088]] ^e51
- Interpretability findings do not provide a complete empirical map of a Latent Program Space, and they support the claims that internal computation is structured, that different pathways contribute to different behaviours and that interventions on representations can change outputs systematically. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0090]] ^e52
- Recognising a table, interpreting its layout or extracting values from noisy source material may require probabilistic model behaviour, while arithmetic relations can be recomputed deterministically once values have been represented explicitly. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0092]] ^e53
- A recurring workflow pattern runs from probabilistic interpretation through structured representation to deterministic checking. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0093]] ^e54
- Deterministic invariants such as row and column totals do not prove that the original source was interpreted correctly and can expose internal inconsistencies and focus subsequent human inspection. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0095]] ^e55
- Model spec midtraining is a proposed technique in which a model is trained, after broad pretraining and before later alignment training, on synthetic documents that discuss a model specification, with the objective of influencing how subsequent alignment training generalises. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0097]] ^e56
- At inference time prompts and context condition and combine parts of the trained repertoire for a particular task, and a harness extends the effective system by providing tools, state, feedback, permissions and control for agentic work. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0099]] ^e57
- The assistant encountered in a conversational interface should not be equated with the underlying neural network, because providers shape assistant behaviour through training, runtime instructions, policy layers and product design. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0100]] ^e58
- Models are extensively trained on human communication and can generate convincing social behaviour, and social fluency, confidence and empathy are no evidence that the system is a human interlocutor or that its claims are correct. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0102]] ^e59
- Three layers should be separated in the shaping of assistant behaviour, training artefacts such as a constitution or model specification, character training and posttraining, and system prompts that operate at runtime within a deployment context. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0104]] ^e60
- Assistant behaviour is produced by the interaction between trained model parameters and current runtime context. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0105]] ^e61
- No single prompting strategy performs optimally across all models, tasks and evaluation settings, so effective prompting is better understood as a combination of several practices than as the discovery of one universal prompt. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0106]] ^e62
- Counter-sycophancy as a prompting practice requires evidence, alternatives or independent critical review where agreement with the user could bias the result. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0111]] ^e63
- The practice of generate, compare and reconcile produces independent candidates, inspects their differences and adjudicates the result. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0112]] ^e64
- Effective prompting combines context selection, instruction design, inference strategy and evaluation, which already moves beyond the prompt itself. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0113]] ^e65
- Complex knowledge work contains more relevant information than should be placed into a single prompt, because goals, documents, data, policies, requirements, examples, design decisions, previous findings, unresolved questions and validation criteria may all matter without mattering at the same time. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0114]] ^e66
- The three layers can be separated by their guiding questions, what the project or organisation knows, what the model or agent needs now, and what the model or agent should do now. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0117]] ^e67
- The shift of Knowledge Engineering is from files to maintained knowledge. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0123]] ^e68
- Information in a document collection may be duplicated, contradictory, outdated, locally structured or understandable only through tacit background knowledge, so Knowledge Engineering concerns acquisition, structuring, curation, provenance, revision and governance rather than storage alone. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0124]] ^e69
- Knowledge can exist without being explicitly documented, shared or usable by an AI agent, because experts rely on tacit distinctions, remembered decisions, local file structures, disciplinary conventions and experience that are not represented in an inspectable form. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0126]] ^e70
- A model cannot reliably use information that remains only in someone's memory and an agent cannot reconstruct project conventions that were never documented, so the question is whether relevant knowledge has been externalised in a usable and revisable form. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0127]] ^e71
- Knowledge Engineering starts from the gap between knowledge possessed and knowledge operationally available. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0128]] ^e72
- Persistent project knowledge lets humans and LLM-based systems refer to the same documented state, so statements can be criticised, updated and linked to evidence while changes remain visible through versioning. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0130]] ^e73
- The form of Knowledge Engineering developed in the notes draws on classical knowledge modelling, on Personal Information Management as the study of how people acquire, organise, maintain, retrieve, use and share information across formats and locations, and on project management. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0133]] ^e74
- A maintained knowledge environment may contain conceptual definitions, source references, requirements, decisions, process descriptions, instructions, open questions and evaluation criteria, because AI-supported knowledge work needs domain representations, usable information collections and operational project state at once. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0134]] ^e75
- Context Engineering extends Prompt Engineering from the design of an immediate instruction to the wider informational environment in which the instruction is interpreted, concerning what information is available, how it is represented, in which order it appears, which resources can be retrieved and what is deliberately omitted. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0139]] ^e76
- The core principle of Context Engineering is selection, the right information in the right representation at the right time. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0141]] ^e77
- The context window is the technical capacity while the context is the information currently represented within it, and in-context learning names the ability of a model to adapt its behaviour to instructions, examples and information supplied in the context without changing model parameters. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0143]] ^e78
- The Working Context is the task-specific configuration of information, documents, data access, instructions, tools and current state made available for a particular assignment, and not every component must be serialised into tokens. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0144]] ^e79
- Context Rot should not be reduced to one established causal mechanism, because position effects, distractors, conflicting information, obsolete intermediate state and the structure of the task can all contribute to degraded long-context performance. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0146]] ^e80
- Distillation is task-dependent and epistemically consequential, because the same source material is distilled differently for a general introduction, an implementation specification and a verification task, and every reduction determines which distinctions remain available for subsequent work. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0150]] ^e81
- Markdown represents document structure directly in human-readable plain text with comparatively little syntax, which makes the structure visible both to humans and to language models. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0151]] ^e82
- Markdown is not intrinsically superior to richer formats, because encoding standards, structured data formats, databases and schemas remain preferable where their semantics or formal constraints are required. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0153]] ^e83
- Useful knowledge documents are bounded, structured, revisable, dual-readable and compact but sufficient, where compactness reduces redundancy without removing distinctions necessary for the intended purpose. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0155]] ^e84
- The knowledge document is the concept and Markdown is one possible serialization. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0156]] ^e85
- An agent can use tools to inspect complete datasets or files while only summaries, query results or selected excerpts enter the active token sequence. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0159]] ^e86
- The user story format makes three questions explicit, who is acting, what capability is required and why the capability matters, which connects implementation requirements to scholarly practice. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0165]] ^e87
- The data model alone does not determine the operations a researcher needs, because requirements arise from the relation among data, research questions and scholarly practices. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0166]] ^e88
- A persona can be used as a structured perspective for evaluation or requirements work, defining a consistent set of characteristics from which an artefact or scenario should be inspected. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0167]] ^e89
- Capture, curation, understanding and exploration require different representations and different working contexts, because each attends to different problems of provenance, entity and relation decisions, domain interpretation and analytical operations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0171]] ^e90
- An AI-supported knowledge environment cannot be reduced to a single prompt or one static database, because different phases of work require different combinations of data, knowledge, tools and validation. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0172]] ^e91
- Project-specific requirements often cannot be completely specified before implementation, and a provisional artefact can make assumptions and possibilities visible, reveal limitations of the underlying data and allow researchers to compare alternative operationalisations. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0174]] ^e92
- Implementation becomes part of enquiry, and domain experts remain responsible for the consequential judgments through which requirements, representations and resulting artefacts are assessed. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0175]] ^e93
- Observations, errors and new questions produced by implementation do not automatically become project knowledge, and they are inspected and, where warranted, written back into the maintained knowledge base. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0177]] ^e94
- The prompt is one operational component inside the knowledge loop rather than its persistent centre. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0179]] ^e95
- Scholar-Centred Design does not imply that every design preference of a researcher should be implemented literally, and its purpose is to make the relation between scholarly questions, data affordances and digital operations explicit enough to be inspected and negotiated. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0181]] ^e96
- Spec-driven development places an explicit specification between exploratory discussion and implementation, developing a structured account of requirements, user stories, data constraints, interfaces and verification criteria before substantial implementation begins. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0183]] ^e97
- An explicit specification reduces the amount of tacit interpretation delegated to the agent and creates a clearer object for review before code or other artefacts are produced. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0185]] ^e98
- The principal shift of Agentic Engineering is from a response to a trajectory. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0188]] ^e99
- An agentic system produces a trajectory containing observations, intermediate decisions, tool calls, file modifications, execution results and subsequent actions, so reliability depends on the organisation of the complete trajectory rather than on a single response. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0189]] ^e100
- Agentic Engineering is about engineering trajectories rather than answers. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0192]] ^e101
- Extended agentic work creates organisational requirements, tasks must be bounded and decomposed, tools, permissions and stopping conditions defined, intermediate results inspected and where necessary escalated to people, and project state must remain understandable across several steps. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0194]] ^e102
- Agentic capability is a new engineering surface in which context, state, tools, control and evaluation have to be coordinated over time rather than simply more autonomy. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0195]] ^e103
- A capable model in a weak harness may be unable to inspect or verify the consequences of its actions, while tools, tests, persistent state and well-designed feedback can make the complete system more capable and more inspectable than model evaluation alone would suggest. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0198]] ^e104
- The concept of an intelligent agent predates large language models by decades, and classical work defines agents through their relationship with an environment and their capacity for autonomous, reactive and goal-directed action. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0199]] ^e105
- Classical agent theory identifies the properties of autonomy, reactivity, proactiveness and social ability, and earlier AI systems demonstrate that agency is not intrinsically tied to language models. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0200]] ^e106
- LLMs changed the practical design space of agents because natural language, code and heterogeneous digital resources can now become part of a common interface for planning and action. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0201]] ^e107
- LLM-based agents should be understood as a contemporary form of a much older AI concept. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0202]] ^e108
- The distinction between chatbot and agent does not require complete autonomy, since contemporary agents normally operate inside human-defined goals, tools, permissions and stopping conditions, and what matters is that the model participates in selecting and coordinating multiple actions. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0208]] ^e109
- A systems perspective treats the harness as the runtime substrate that enables a foundation model to operate as an agent, and software engineering capability arises from a model, harness and environment system rather than from the model in isolation. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0209]] ^e110
- A harness can manage task specification, context selection, file and tool access, action execution, current state, feedback, permissions, verification and intervention points, which changes what should be evaluated, because a final patch, document or answer provides only partial evidence about the process that produced it. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0210]] ^e111
- A stronger harness can preserve execution traces, test results, failure information and other evidence that makes an agentic trajectory inspectable and easier to continue or correct. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0211]] ^e112
- Tool use, harnesses, instruction files, agent skills, tool protocols, agent-to-agent protocols and subagents solve different problems and should not be treated as interchangeable labels, so the engineering task is to decide which mechanism carries which type of information or capability. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0213]] ^e113
- Tools matter especially when they return evidence about the consequences of an action, since a compiler, test suite or executable environment can provide deterministic feedback on proposed code and a schema validator can determine conformance of a proposed document. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0215]] ^e114
- Tool use changes the epistemic structure of the workflow, because the system is no longer relying only on model-generated text and can obtain external observations that constrain subsequent actions. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0216]] ^e115
- Information that should guide an agent repeatedly should not have to be typed into every prompt, and persistent instruction artefacts externalise stable working rules and make them inspectable and versionable. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0219]] ^e116
- A global or user-level instruction file should contain information that is genuinely stable across projects, while project-specific facts remain in the project environment rather than being duplicated globally. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0221]] ^e117
- An instruction file is not a hard security mechanism, and behavioural guidance is distinguished from permissions and hooks, which can enforce technical boundaries independently of model compliance. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0222]] ^e118
- Instruction files are treated as context rather than infallible configuration, so they need to remain concise, specific and internally consistent, and procedural content relevant only for particular tasks may be better represented as a skill than loaded in every session. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0225]] ^e119
- An agent skill is a reusable folder containing a skill definition and optionally scripts, references and assets for a particular capability or workflow, and skills are procedural artefacts rather than merely descriptive knowledge documents. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0226]] ^e120
- The agent skill specification uses progressive disclosure, so at session start the agent receives only the name and description of available skills, the full instructions are loaded when the skill becomes relevant and additional resources are loaded later as required. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0227]] ^e121
- Progressive disclosure helps control context consumption, since a system can maintain many specialised capabilities without injecting all instructions into every task. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0228]] ^e122
- A tool protocol server can expose resources, prompts and tools that a compatible client discovers and invokes through standardised messages, so the protocol addresses an integration problem rather than a reasoning or knowledge modelling problem. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0230]] ^e123
- An agent-to-agent protocol supports agent discovery, task management and the exchange of information or results without requiring one agent to expose its internal memory, tools or proprietary implementation. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0232]] ^e124
- Tool protocols and agent-to-agent protocols address complementary interaction surfaces, one standardising how an agent interacts with tools and resources, the other how independent agents collaborate as peers. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0233]] ^e125
- Multi-agent interoperability increases coordination and verification requirements, because more agents create more possible handoffs, divergent assumptions and failure points. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0234]] ^e126
- A subagent is a delegated agent instance for a bounded part of a larger task that operates with its own fresh context, inspects a defined subset of resources and returns a compact result to the parent agent. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0235]] ^e127
- Delegation to a subagent protects the parent context from large volumes of intermediate information and allows independent subagents to execute parallelisable checks or analyses. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0236]] ^e128
- Subagents are an architectural pattern rather than a universally standardised protocol, their value depends on task decomposition, information boundaries and the quality of the returned evidence, and delegation does not remove the need to validate results. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0237]] ^e129
- Agentic workflows can route different phases of work to different models, because planning, implementation and review do not necessarily require the same model or the same inference budget. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0238]] ^e130
- Planning and review can be assigned to a model selected for stronger reasoning while implementation is assigned to a model with a favourable balance of coding capability, latency and cost, and the resulting specification remains contextualised by project knowledge. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0241]] ^e131
- Model routing is an engineering decision that can change as models evolve, and the important abstraction is the separation of functions and evaluation criteria rather than a particular provider combination. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0242]] ^e132
- Additional agents do not create truth by voting, and the evidence returned by schemas, tests, source comparisons and domain-specific knowledge remains more important than model agreement by itself. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0244]] ^e133
- The purpose of multi-agent orchestration is to create structured trajectories of independent work and feedback rather than to multiply model calls. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0245]] ^e134
- Long-running work accumulates intermediate outputs, errors, tool results and previous decisions, and if all of this remains in one undifferentiated context the agent may spend substantial effort navigating its own history rather than solving the current task. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0246]] ^e135
- Stable knowledge can remain outside the transient conversation, relevant parts can be loaded or retrieved when needed, and completed stages can produce compact artefacts that later stages consume rather than carrying every intermediate token forward. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0247]] ^e136
- Evaluation is the broader measurement or assessment of outputs, models or workflows against explicit criteria and may be quantitative or qualitative, including benchmark scores, error rates, task completion measures or structured expert review. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0255]] ^e137
- Technical verification asks whether an artefact conforms to specified or formalised requirements and can often be automated because the relevant conditions have been formalised. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0256]] ^e138
- Scholarly or domain validation asks whether a representation, interpretation or artefact is adequate to the source, purpose and disciplinary context, so an encoded document can be technically valid while still encoding a historically unjustified interpretation. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0257]] ^e139
- Acceptance is the responsible decision to use an identified state for a specified purpose, and an artefact may be verified and validated to a particular degree while still requiring an explicit decision about whether the available evidence suffices for publication, analysis or operational use. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0258]] ^e140
- AI-supported work changes where expertise is applied rather than removing the need for it, because experts define purposes, externalise relevant knowledge, establish modelling distinctions, determine constraints, design evaluation criteria and decide how evidence should affect the status of an output. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0259]] ^e141
- A Critical Expert is not merely a person who manually checks everything a model produces, and the role is to design and maintain the epistemic conditions under which model-generated and deterministic outputs can be interpreted, including provenance, validation rules, acceptance criteria and procedures for handling uncertainty. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0260]] ^e142
- The complete system can be understood as an epistemic infrastructure in which knowledge documents, source data, schemas, tests, software tools, AI agents, project state and human judgment work together, so the central question is under which technical and epistemic conditions an output can be inspected, justified and used. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0261]] ^e143
- The practical significance of contemporary frontier LLMs for knowledge work lies in the interaction of capable models with explicit knowledge, managed context, tools, executable environments, feedback and responsible human judgment rather than in a single prompting technique. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0262]] ^e144
- The resulting shift is from individual model outputs towards the design of complete technical and epistemic environments, which does not eliminate human responsibility and increases the importance of making assumptions, provenance, transformations, verification, validation and acceptance inspectable within the workflow. [[10_markdown/documents/lecture-notes-en#^p0264]] ^e145

## Open questions

- The notes state that the boundaries and terminology of the training stages differ across laboratories without naming a criterion by which a reader could assign a given published pipeline to them.
- Asymmetric amplification is introduced as the author's own description of a practical effect, and the notes name no measurement by which the asymmetry could be quantified for a given research setting.
- The Latent Program Space account is presented as a theoretical model supported by partial interpretability findings, and the notes leave open which observations would count against it.
- The notes describe evaluation, technical verification, scholarly validation and acceptance as conceptually distinct without stating how a project records which of the four has been performed on a given artefact.
- The relation between the prose headings of this version and the numbered chapters of the German version is stated in neither source.

## Appraisal

The English notes are the more theoretically explicit of the two versions and the only feeding artefact that gives a connected account of model internals from tokenisation to interpretability. Their strength for this vault is that they consistently mark the epistemic status of their own claims, distinguishing a proposed technique from an established one, a metaphor from a mechanism and a laboratory's self-description from a finding. That discipline is what allows a book chapter to reuse them without inheriting overclaims. Their limit is the same as the German version's, they cite the research literature in passing rather than examining it, and several of the load-bearing terms, asymmetric amplification and the Latent Program Space reading among them, are the author's own proposals rather than established results. Chapters that lean on those terms have to name them as the author's, and the underlying publications belong in the reference layer before an assertion rests on them alone.
