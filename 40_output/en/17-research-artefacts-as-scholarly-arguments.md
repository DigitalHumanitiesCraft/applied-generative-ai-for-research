# Research Artefacts as Scholarly Arguments

## What an artefact is

A digital research artefact is a project-specific operational form through which researchers work with structured research data for a defined scholarly purpose, and it is distinguished from the data by that function rather than by its medium.[^1] The forms it takes vary widely, covering models, pipelines, interfaces, transformations, editions, verification environments, and applications, and no particular software form is constitutive, because what recurs is the relation between maintained project knowledge and an artefact that makes selected scholarly operations available for examination.[^2]

That such artefacts display research data is the least interesting thing about them. Software operationalises only what has been encoded in structures its representational model can process, so every artefact enacts a prior selection of what is representable at all.[^3] Digital tool criticism has established the consequence for scholarship, in that software participates in knowledge production rather than serving as a neutral means of access, and generic tools carry predefined representations and forms of interaction that may not correspond to a project's data and practices, which pushes researchers to adapt their inquiry to assumptions developed elsewhere.[^4] A project-specific artefact does not escape selection. What it changes is where the selection is made and whether it can be inspected, since the choices are taken in relation to the project's own data and purpose and stay examinable there.[^5]

Formal preparation does not settle any of this in advance. Modelling and standards support machine-actionability, interoperability, and reuse, and they leave open how researchers should work with the data in a particular project, because the same data may support different research questions and therefore require different operational forms, which the two contrasting investigations built on one shared model of historical accounting transactions demonstrate.[^6]

## Where the interpretation sits

The interpretative content of an artefact lies in its structures and its interactions. Which entities become nodes, whether an edge is directed, how observations are aggregated, whether missing values remain visible, how temporal uncertainty is represented, which categories are selectable, and which comparisons are enabled together fix the forms of inquiry the artefact supports and the forms it forecloses.[^7]

The clearest documented instance concerns uncertainty that the data themselves already encode. Dates recorded with an earliest and a latest bound were shown as precise points on a timeline, which asserted a precision the records did not carry, and representing them as intervals restored what the encoding had preserved. The correction belonged to the visual and operational treatment of uncertainty and left the underlying records untouched.[^8] Interaction with developing interfaces has repeatedly produced findings of this shape, revealing where distinctions could be supported by the available material, where categories remained ambiguous, where capture required further qualification, and where a technically possible visualisation would imply a degree of precision, completeness, or certainty the data could not warrant.[^9]

A functioning implementation is therefore not necessarily a scholarly adequate one. Technical verification asks whether an output conforms to formalised requirements, while scholarly validation asks whether the representation those requirements encode is warranted by the source material and adequate for its purpose, so an implementation can be correct and inadequate at once.[^10] The same asymmetry appears one layer down in transformations, where an output may conform to its mapping and be schema-valid while representing the source inadequately, and where an error in the implementation of an adequate mapping is corrected in the transformation rather than in the mapping.[^11] Technical success can coexist with unsupported categories, inappropriate mappings, misleading visual conventions, and concealed uncertainty.

## The critique of tool positivism

Tool positivism is the assumption that an adequate technical implementation is itself an adequate solution to the underlying scholarly problem.[^12] It is an easy assumption to slide into, because the evidence it offers is immediate. The artefact runs, the tests pass, the visualisation renders, and the impression of resolution arrives before anyone has asked what the representation claims.

Against that, the artefact produces no scholarly knowledge on its own. Maintained project knowledge gives provisional form to a project's understanding, implementation translates part of it into a functional artefact, and its epistemic relevance arises through operationalisation, examination, responsible interpretation, attribution of what is found, and write-back into the revised understanding.[^13] Generation and functionality establish nothing by themselves.

What a functional artefact does add is a mode of access that formal notation does not provide. Digital Humanities scholarship has long treated prototypes, interfaces, and visualisations as forms through which interpretative claims and research possibilities can be explored rather than merely presented, and where a schema presents a model through notation, an interface shows how selected distinctions behave when data are entered, filtered, compared, and represented.[^14] Documented episodes exist in which project partners examined categories through such operations rather than through a formal definition alone.[^14]

The claim has to be kept at its actual width. The documented practice does not establish that functional artefacts broaden participation, improve decisions, or produce findings that could not also have emerged through source criticism, specification review, data profiling, non-functional prototypes, or structured discussion, so artefact-mediated scholarly enquiry is a proposition the cases motivate rather than a demonstrated comparative advantage.[^15] Holding that line is itself part of the critique of tool positivism, since a method that overstated the epistemic yield of building things would reproduce the error it names.[^16]
